**_How did you first come up with the idea for this work?_**  
During COVID I sat and watched youtube video and demonstrations on Michael Faraday's _The Chemical History of a Candle_, though dated, the idea of sequential demonstrations using ever day objects leading us to understand more about the world caught my attention. Shifting to soap, a ubiquitous consumer product, I wanted to create a set of experiments readers could perform at home to explore chemistry and materials science.

**_What was your research process like?_**  
I spent a lot of time in libraries and archives across the Science History Institute, New York Public Library, and New York Academy of Medicine looking at old soap ads, patent medicine formulas, and exploring the cultural history of soap. My favorite discovery was on 72nd street when a building was being demolished and it revealed a century old advertisement for a washing powder. A lot of the experiments were also texted in classrooms at FIT where students blew bubbles, made emulsions, and crafted soap from cooking oils.

**_How long did you work on this before it was published?_**  
3 years.

**_Does this work relate to your role at FIT? If so, how?_**  
Yes, in many ways I wrote this book for my students. Science-curious and interested readers who want to know more about the products they use and put on their bodies. Even though the book wanders into stories that don't always make it into the classroom, but writing the book I learned so much about how we explain scientific concepts to a general audience.

**_What was your biggest challenge? What was most rewarding?_**  
The scariest part of the project was starting from an outline and thinking about writing 200+ pages of text, but slow and steady writing, and taking time to read similar texts helped me flesh things out. The most rewarding part was seeing the books reception. From educators, to former mentors, it's an amazing thing to send a text out into the world and see what others think.
